Body Temperature

What chills you, what warms you, why wet clothes are dangerous, and how to recover from the cold.

Cold is slow but relentless: water, rain, wind, night and wet clothes pull your temperature down, and fire, sun, shelter and movement bring it back. Keep dry and you’ll rarely be in trouble.

Reading your temperature

  • 37 °C is normal. The ring is green from 36 °C to 38 °C.
  • Blue (35 °C or below) is a warning, not an emergency. In mild weather with some clothes on, you can settle as low as 34 °C and stay healthy.
  • Below 32 °C is danger. After 25 minutes below 32 °C you start getting sick, or after about 12 minutes if your clothes are wet too. Sickness is what eventually costs you health. See Sickness and Medicine.
  • A roof, a closed vehicle or a safe zone stops the sickness building, even while you’re cold. Once you’re back above 32 °C, or under cover, the exposure clock unwinds twice as fast as it built up.
  • ++ and +++ mean you’ve stored warmth by a fire. Cold eats that store before it touches your temperature.

What makes you colder

  • Being in water. Nothing chills you faster. With no wetsuit you can drop from 37 °C to 32 °C in about 7 minutes, and ordinary clothes are soaked within seconds.
  • Cold air. The air is coldest around 02:00 and warmest around 14:00, in-game. Rain lowers it the most, then heavy cloud, then fog.
  • Night. From 21:00 to 05:00 you lose a little extra heat.
  • Wet clothes. Once you’re Wet or worse, evaporation chills you. In warm air this fades to 15% of its full strength.
  • Wind. Wind makes cold air, rain and wet clothes worse. On a dry, warm day it does nothing.
  • High ground. Every 100 m of height is about 0.65 °C colder.
  • Bleeding. Every open wound adds a chill.
  • Hunger and thirst. With food or water under 10%, your body can’t make heat.

What warms you

  • A lit fire. You get full warmth within 2 m, less further away and nothing beyond 5 m. Any lit fire counts. A fire warms you back to normal but stops once you’re a degree above it. Don’t stand in the flames: see Fires and Cooking.
  • Sunshine. It shines from 05:00 to 21:00, strongest around 13:00. A roof, a closed vehicle, heavy cloud or thick fog blocks it. Bare skin catches more of it than clothes do.
  • Moving. Jogging and sprinting warm you. Walking and riding in a vehicle don’t. Warm clothes hold that heat in. Below 32 °C, movement warms you less, down to half as much at 25 °C.
  • Shelter. Under a roof, the air around you counts as 4 °C warmer and rain stops soaking you. A closed vehicle counts as at least 6 °C warmer, keeps rain and wind off, and cancels the chill of high ground. Motorbikes and other open seats give you none of that.
  • Recovering naturally. Your body drifts back toward normal on its own, faster in warmer air and in dry clothes. With food or water under 30%, it recovers at half speed.
  • Hero perks. On the Hero path you lose up to 30% less heat from every source.

Wet clothes

Wet clothes don’t freeze you by themselves. They stop keeping you warm. Every piece of clothing shows its state in its tooltip:

State Insulation it keeps
Dry All of it
Damp 90%
Wet 75%
Soaked 50%
Drenched None

Drenched clothes are useless, but they never make you colder than wearing nothing.

  • Heavy rain takes you from Dry to Damp in under 20 seconds and to Drenched in about 4 and a half minutes with no rain gear.
  • In water you’re Drenched in about 16 seconds.
  • Air drying takes about 40 minutes from Drenched to Dry on a warm day, and up to five times longer in cold air.
  • By a fire it takes about 3 minutes, whatever the weather. A fire can’t dry you while rain is still landing on you, so get under a roof first.
  • Rain gear also speeds up drying, up to three times with full coverage.

Clothing

Clothing tooltips show an Insulation rating: Poor, Low, Medium, High or Best. Clothing with no insulation shows no rating. Not every slot counts equally:

  • Jacket and trousers: about a quarter of your warmth each.
  • Boots: about an eighth.
  • Headwear: about a tenth.
  • Vest and gloves: a little less than headwear.
  • Backpacks, glasses and everything else: nothing.

Worn-out clothing keeps you less warm, and a ruined piece gives no warmth at all. A strong cold-weather outfit blocks a little over half of the cold.

Rating Examples
Best The ushanka, folded or unfolded. The only Best item.
High Knitted hats, loose beanies, wool gloves, firefighter jacket and trousers, some bomber jackets
Medium Wetsuits, firefighter helmets, hiking boots, other bomber jackets
Low Raincoats, rain jackets, fisherman trousers, wellies, most helmets

Rain gear

A second tooltip line rates how much rain a piece keeps off the part of you it covers:

  • Water resistant: half.
  • Rainproof: most.
  • Waterproof: all.

Coverage adds up across slots. A waterproof raincoat on its own keeps about a quarter of the rain off you. Pair it with a waterproof hat or helmet, fisherman trousers, wellies and waterproof gloves, and about 90% of the rain stays off you. Firefighter trousers are rainproof, not waterproof. A backpack’s rain rating does nothing to keep you dry.

Wetsuits halve the chill of the water, never let you get wetter than Wet, and count as waterproof. Wear one for any long swim.

Stored warmth: ++ and +++

Sitting by a fire once you’re at about normal temperature builds a store of warmth instead of heating you further. Cold drains this store first, before your temperature drops.

  • ++ takes about 7 minutes at the fire, and +++ about 12.
  • Your clothes set the limit. With little on, you can’t store enough to see ++. For +++ you need heavily insulated clothing.
  • The store fades in under half an hour even in mild weather, faster in the cold. Charge it right before you head out.

Overheating

Heat is rarely a danger. Fire and sunshine stop warming you once you’re a degree or so above normal, and your body sheds extra heat quickly. The one thing that pushes you higher is running in heavy clothing, which traps heat and slows sweating. Slow down or take a layer off and you’ll cool back to 37 °C.

Recovering from cold

  1. Get out of the water and the rain. Go under a roof or into a closed vehicle. Sickness stops building there.
  2. Light a fire and stand within 2 m. The fire alone takes about 8 minutes to bring you from 32 °C back to 37 °C, longer in bad weather.
  3. Dry off by the fire. Drenched to Dry takes about 3 minutes, and your clothes start working again.
  4. Eat and drink. Under 30% food or water halves your recovery, and under 10% actively chills you.
  5. Stop any bleeding.
  6. No fire? Keep moving, in dry clothes if you have them.
  7. Charge ++ or +++ before you leave the fire.
